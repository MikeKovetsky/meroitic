**The accepted words: 43, not 83.** The plan counted every entry of the lexicon of 2003 that the lexicon calls

The script holds the list and stops if the map gives another.

- **Offered and absent.** In each kind, every third accepted word by lot is shown without its true meaning: 13
  of the 43. The other 30 are shown with it.
- **The open words: 33,** those of the first test, each with the proposals on record that two readers found for
  it (one to three). Their places are those the map with its four layers still holds open, three at least.
- **Four options a word.** A word's own meanings (the true one, or the proposals) are filled up to four by lot
  from one pool: the 43 accepted meanings and the proposals of the open words, those of the word's kind first.
  In the first test every filler was an accepted meaning, short and plain, and the proposals stood out by their
  wording. Now the two sorts stand mixed under every word.
- **Never two near meanings.** A filler shares no content word with another option of its word, nor with the
  true meaning of a word shown without it. Seventeen groups of meanings that no context could tell apart are
  kept apart too (bull and cow; to give, to present and to grant; good, abundant and beneficent; and so on):
  set by hand before any reader, they are in the script.
- **What a reader sees:** as in the first test. Up to twelve places of the hidden word, six words on each side
  in the map's reading, no spelling, no name of an inscription. The readings are those of the map with its four
  layers; a piece's reading is shown without the spelling of its whole word. The brief is that of the first test,
  with the number of lines the pack expects added.
- **The readers:** five for each of 5 packs, each a fresh agent (Opus): 25. The lots are drawn with the seed
  20261122.

## 3. The bars, set before any reader is started

1. Every reader answers every word of its pack.
2. **A choice needs all five readers:** the same option from each ("none of these" is one). In the first test
   four of five were enough.
3. Of the accepted words shown with their true meaning for which the readers make a choice other than "none",
   at least 80% are right, and there are at least 8 such words.
4. Of the accepted words shown without their true meaning, at most 10% get a choice other than "none" (of 13:
   one at most).
5. If a bar is missed, no label is given.

Bars 3 and 4 are those of the first test, unchanged; only what counts as a choice is stricter.

## 4. What an open word then is

- **favoured by the contexts:** the bars hold, and all five readers pick the same proposal;
- **chosen, no label:** all five pick the same proposal, but a bar is missed;
- **none of the four,** **a filler chosen,** or **no choice** otherwise.

A label is not a translation. The map and the counts of the scoreboard stay as they are whatever the result;
whether a favoured proposal should ever count there would change what a row means, and that is Mike's call.

## 5. What the test cannot show

- **It is not a test on new words.** 38 of the 43 accepted words are those of the first test, and the rule "all
  five" was read off that test's answers. Fresh readers, a new draw of the places shown, of the fillers and of
  the words that go without their meaning make this a second measurement of the same words. The 5 new words are
  reported apart; they are too few for a bar of their own.
- **The accepted words are the easy ones:** frequent, and long understood. A rate measured on them is the most
  that can be hoped for an open word, not what holds for it.
- **A proposal favoured over three fillers** is favoured over those three, not over every meaning a word could
  have.
- **The readers are one model.** It may know published translations in spite of the brief, and five readers
  that agree are five draws from one reader.
- No meaning found here is the project's own.

The seven are `wse` (of the country), `wi` (brother), `yed` (silver), `pqrtr` (great prince), `ẖol` (to grant),
`qomlise` (good god) and `penn` (year). They carry no label. Six of them had all five readers of the first test
too, five with the same proposal; for `ẖol` the readers then agreed on "to present" and now on "to grant", two
of its three proposals.

**What this shows.**

- **With the right meaning among the four, five readers who agree are nearly always right.** That part of the
  first test held, and more strongly: 16 of 17, against 12 of 15.
- **Without it, they agree on a wrong one about one time in four.** The first test's "no wrong choice among 11"
  did not hold: it was read off the answers after the count, and this is what such a figure is worth.
- **An open word is in the second case whenever its true meaning is not among the proposals,** and nobody knows
  when that is. So a proposal that all five choose cannot be told from a wrong one chosen for want of the right
  one. "Silver" shows it: all five chose it for `yed`, where it is the proposal on record, and all five chose it
  for the word for "god", where it was a filler.
- The method as it stands, a choice among four meanings from the contexts alone, is not a test a proposal can
  pass. This is its second miss, and the stricter rule did not mend it.
