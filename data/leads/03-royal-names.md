# Lead 3: Bilingual royal names, titles and divine names

**Answer first.** This lead is real but already spent on phonology: Griffith set most of the sound values in
1909-1911 from exactly these names. It yields about 8 royal names or royal titles written in both Egyptian
(hieroglyphic or Demotic) or Greek and in Meroitic, and about 6 divine names. It adds almost no vocabulary
beyond `qore` "ruler", `kdke`/`ktke` "Candace" and the name element `-amani` "Amun". Its use for a new attempt
is as a check on vowel and consonant values (for example Egyptian *Ndkꜣmn* against Meroitic *Ntkmni*).
The fullest list is the "Titles" entries of FHN II and III, which give every Egyptian and Meroitic writing
of each ruler's name with REM numbers.

## Griffith's own account of the starting point

Griffith 1911, *Karanog: the Meroitic inscriptions of Shablul and Karanog*, Preface pp. v-vi
([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/karangmeroitic00grifuoft), public domain):

> A clear starting-point was furnished by the bilingual hieroglyphic cartouches of King Natakamani and Queen
> Amanitere, and by the name of Ammon accompanying the figure of the god in the hieroglyphic inscriptions of
> Naga. The importance of these had long ago been recognized by Lepsius; they indicated the sounds of seven
> or eight letters, and proved that the values of the Meroitic hieroglyphs were in part taken from Egyptian.

He goes on to name three more "script bilinguals" (same language, two scripts), which fixed the cursive
values:

1. Inscr. 60, a funerary text in Meroitic hieroglyphs found in Berlin in 1908, "parallel to those in
   demotic" (that is, Meroitic cursive).
2. Inscr. 126, a cursive stela "naming Natakamani and Amanitere" communicated by Golenischeff; FHN III gives it
   as REM 0126, Moscow, Pushkin Museum.
3. Meroe 1, a cursive caption of the lion god from Garstang's excavations, matching the hieroglyphic captions
   of the lion temple at Naga.

## The Wad ban Naqa barque stand

FHN III (211), p. 896: Natakamani's names are on "a barque stand from the temple of Isis at Wad ban Naqa,
Berlin 7261, Griffith 1911a, Pls XXIV f., REM 0041", repeated on two smaller stands and an altar fragment
from the same site (REM 0040). FHN's footnote 530 specifies that on this stand the Throne name is in Egyptian
hieroglyphs and the Son of Rê name in Meroitic hieroglyphs, so the stand pairs the two scripts on one object
rather than writing one name twice. The Egyptian writing of the Son of Rê name comes from Gebel Barkal (below).
Griffith 1911a (*Meroitic Inscriptions I*, plates XXIV-XXV) is not on archive.org.

Diodorus 3.6.3, translated by C. H. Oldfather (Loeb 1935), from [LacusCurtius, Diodorus book 3](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diodorus_Siculus/3A*.html):

> but during the reign of the second Ptolemy the king of the Ethiopians, Ergamenes, who had had a Greek
> education and had studied philosophy, was the first to have the courage to disdain the command.

The Greek ending *-amenes* renders the Kushite element *-amani* "Amun"; Griffith 1911 p. 8 (c) cites
"the Ethiopian-Meroitic pronunciation shown in -amane, -αμενης" for the name of Amun.

## Where to mine more pairs

Each FHN "Titles" entry lists every Egyptian and Meroitic writing of a ruler's names, with REM numbers. Not
all were read for this note:

   p. 904; (214) Arkhatani p. 907; (215) Shorakaror p. 908; (216) Amanitaraqide p. 912; (217) Amanitenmomide

   Tamelordeamani p. 1048; (276) Yesebokheamani p. 1049 (his Meroitic graffiti at Philae are REM 0119-0120).
3. The Naga Apedemak (lion) temple, with Egyptian hieroglyphic and Meroitic hieroglyphic captions side by

   graffito of Qeren, son of Wayekiye (A) and Taêse, is in the same temple (Zibelius 1983, 38-40, cited by
   Ashby p. 143 n. 75).
