Meroitic, the language of ancient Kush, can be read aloud but is mostly not understood. Over a night and a morning, about 60 Opus 5.5 subagents tested what the model already knows, what it can work out from the texts alone, what new leads it finds for specialists, and whether it can grow the corpus. A third day read 151 more inscriptions from hand copies and tested the open leads on texts the model had never seen.

Corpus: Otten & Anastasopoulos's public corpus (ALP 2025), cleaned. Answer key: 82 published word meanings and 26 grammatical endings, each sourced. Code, prompts and every answer file are in the meroitic repo.

Did we find anything new?

No decipherment. New: the public corpus is mostly copies, 303 inscriptions are machine-readable under an open licence, and a test that works

  1. The public Meroitic corpus is mostly copies. The only machine-readable corpus is a file of 18,090 lines that holds 662 different texts or phrases: each is repeated, about 27 times on average, with only a person's name changed. The short label tdxe mlEqErebr qEret ___ appears 198 times, once with each of 198 names. Counting words in the file counts the same texts over and over, and some titles come out about 100 times too frequent. The paper describes the name swapping (871 examples made into 17,257); the released file does not say it is that version. We recovered the swapped name lists from the data and undid it.
  2. 303 inscriptions are now machine-readable under an open licence, 237 of them missing from that corpus. Opus reads the Meroitic type printed in Griffith's public-domain editions of 1911–12 at 95.6% sign accuracy against the modern edition, measured on 12 cursive texts; about a third of the rows are in hieroglyphic type and were not scored. It transcribed 152 of his inscriptions, each with its source page, under CC BY 4.0; 86 were not in the corpus. His hand copies of the Karanòg and Shablûl epitaphs it reads at 89% on their own, which is not enough. But the same book prints most of their words again in type, and with each reading checked against those pages it reaches 97.7% on the four texts that have a modern edition: 151 more inscriptions, none of them in the corpus. A second reader of eleven of the 53 pages got 97.7% of the same signs. Photographs of the stones are beyond it.
  3. Scrambling the script doesn't hide it from a frontier model. From a 17-word glossary of letter-swapped Meroitic and no texts, it broke the cipher and matched all 35 test words. From the scrambled texts with neutral labels, it recognised the opening Wos-i Sorey-i ("O Isis, O Osiris") and read the script from there. Tests of AI decipherment on known-but-undeciphered scripts have to assume the model has read the literature.
  4. A test that works, and what it shows. On a synthetic twin language with every word invented, it recovered 41 and 43 of 52 meanings on two runs, where chance is about 2. On real words it could not recall, the corpus got it to Rilly's meaning for 13 of 35, and 20 within three guesses.
  5. Its confidence is a usable filter. In every test, all 74 answers it gave at confidence 4 or 5 were right. At confidence 1 or 2, about a third were.
  6. It restores lost words about twice as well as statistics. Where a masked word has no parallel anywhere in the texts, it restored 48% on the first guess; an n-gram model restored 26%.
  7. What did not hold up. None of the 14 "new" readings its discovery runs proposed survived a check designed to prove them wrong: 3 were already in print, 11 weakened, 2 partial leads remain. With open word lists, Meroitic shows no resemblance to Nubian beyond the 20 or so word series Rilly built his sound laws from.

Leads for specialists

What the discovery runs proposed, and what survived a second check

Three runs read the funerary texts, the Hamadab stela and the Philae inscriptions, searched the literature, and labelled 7 ideas as new. A fourth run then tried to prove each one wrong, with harder searches in English, French and German, the FHN commentary and Millet's 1968 glossary. None survived as a clean new finding: 2 were already published and 5 were weakened. The model finds real patterns, but where the key books are print-only it cannot tell a rediscovery from a discovery. The two leads left open were then tested on 185 texts typed afterwards, which played no part in finding them: one got weaker and one held.

weakened
Tanyidamani

wi before a numeral marks a total: mk lte wi 12 follows exactly twelve items each counted "1". That sum stands, but the reading got weaker on unseen texts. On REM 1003 the counts of men and women add up to 648 (747 at most) and the number after wi is 3348 in every edition we hold, so the sum Hofmann 1981 reported there could not be reproduced. In the Tabo accounts wi stands before one numeral after another, once before a count of 1. "X are N" (Hallof 2022) fits better than "total".

held
funerary

Rilly has published one address ending -i placed after the pair "Isis + Osiris". The corpus suggests the same for "god + epithet": Osiris is never Soreyi before an epithet in 11 texts. But Isis keeps her -i before an epithet in one family. On the unseen texts it held: 9 of 9 for Osiris and 12 of 12 for Isis. The count cannot tell a moved vocative from two fixed formulas, each copied whole. Check Rilly & de Voogt 2012, p. 155, and Heyler 1964.

weakened
Philae

The proposal that the Faras stela REM 0129 repeats envoy Sasan's title chain from Ph. 416 fails. A word masked as a name there is really the title womnis, so the stela lists five or more titles. FHN III already pairs REM 0129 with Sasan, and the order matches ordinary rank order.

weakened
Philae

The proposal that "general of the water" in Ph. 417 belongs to Bekemete's father was already made: Griffith 1911 set Ph. 417 beside REM 0098, and Török 2009 reportedly gives the father the title.

weakened
Hamadab

The proposal that the totals 1,626 and 1,032 on REM 1003 count animals fails: the related stela REM 1041 counts 1,032 men (abr wi 1032).

published
funerary

The title plSn from Egyptian pꜣ mr-šn, the lesonis: already in FHN III and Ashby 2016.

published
three runs

penn as a numbered year: Millet 1968 already reads penn 34 as "regnal year 34". Three runs rediscovered it independently, which at least shows the pattern is plain in the texts.

published
Philae

Supported here: tebwe is the Abaton (Bigeh), not Thebes (Griffith 1911, Ashby 2016), and ste can name a man, so Rilly's "tutor" fits better than "mother".

negative
Hamadab

REM 1003, the British Museum stela, contains nothing that can be checked against the war with Rome: no Roman place names, and no number that matches a Roman figure. This agrees with Rilly 2014, who places the war in the twin stela REM 1039.

Full write-ups with evidence lines and searches: data/discovery/funerary.md, philae.md, hamadab.md, and the adversarial check verification.md.

Corrections found along the way

Problems in the data and the received picture

  • The public corpus is padded. Its 18,090 lines repeat each text with person names swapped from two pools (178 private names, 11 royal). Undone, it is 662 texts and about 18,100 words; the funerary part is 442 distinct texts in 306 families. The three royal narratives were also in it twice. Word counts from the raw file overstate some titles about 100-fold.
  • "Arme = Rome" is withdrawn. Rilly wrote in 2014 that armil and armeyose "have nothing to do with Rome". The Romans appear as Tmey in REM 1039, and Arome (Rome) is attested only in 3rd-century texts.
  • The "Hamadab stela" file is REM 1003, the British Museum stela EA 1650, also called the Akinidad stela. Its eroded twin in Khartoum is REM 1039.
  • Glossary traps. On the war stela, kdise means "each woman", not "sister", and wi before a number is not "brother".
What it would take

Where a model helps from here

  • Data first. Hallof's 2024 corpus edition exists only in print, and the REM volumes online give photographs and drawings, not text (as Otten's paper describes them). A clean digital corpus with REM numbers, line breaks and doubtful signs marked would do more than any model upgrade. Two printed sources were read here on the third day: Griffith's Karanòg (151 texts, public) and the Meroitic Newsletter (256 texts, in copyright, kept private).
  • Use it as a tireless analyst, scored on held-out material. The synthetic test shows its confidence is well calibrated on clean data. Its high-confidence readings of unknown words are worth a specialist's time; its low-confidence ones are not.
  • A real cognate test. Apply Rilly's sound correspondences to the full comparison series in his 2003 thesis appendix (about 200 series), not raw string similarity.
  • Pair it with a specialist from the start. The lead that held, and any future "new" label, need someone with Rilly 2007, Hintze and the REM volumes on the shelf: Claude Rilly (CNRS), Jochen Hallof, Solange Ashby or Joshua Otten.
  • Digitize the reference shelf too. Its false "new" labels came from print-only sources it could not search: Millet 1968, Hofmann 1981, FHN commentary pages. Searchable copies of those would have caught them.
  • Four builds come before more runs. A corpus with one row per inscription and real names; a lexicon with a source and a status for every meaning; one harness for all the tests, run on more than one model; and whole texts translated and scored against printed renderings. Section F of the next steps defines each.
Methods, data, code and every answer file: the meroitic repo. Every claim names its run in REPORT.md. Eight independent full code reviews checked the scripts.
